## Millimeter-scale LED light sources that fly through space

### Toward light-emitting pixels for touchable mid-air displays

**1. Authors:**

- Yoshihiro Kawahara (Associate Professor, Department of Information and Communication Engineering, Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, The University of Tokyo; Research Director, JST ERATO Kawahara Universal Information Network Project)
- Makoto Takamiya (Associate Professor, VLSI Design and Education Center, The University of Tokyo)
- Yasuaki Kakehi (Associate Professor, Faculty of Environment and Information Studies, Keio University)
- Takayuki Hoshi (Visiting Researcher, Research Center for Advanced Science and Technology, The University of Tokyo)

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**2. Key points:**

- We developed a tiny LED (Note 2) light source, 4 millimeters in diameter, that levitates and moves in mid-air using a focused ultrasonic beam (Note 1).
- Two innovations made it small and light enough to be lifted by the weak force of ultrasound: eliminating the battery with wireless power transfer (Note 3), and integrating the wireless-power receiver circuit needed to light the LED into a dedicated IC (Note 4).
- The movement of the tiny LED light source through space and its switching on and off can be controlled wirelessly from a computer, and it is expected to serve in the future as a light-emitting pixel for touchable mid-air displays.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**3. Summary:**  
A research group led by Associate Professor Makoto Takamiya, Associate Professor Yoshihiro Kawahara and Visiting Researcher Takayuki Hoshi of the University of Tokyo, and Associate Professor Yasuaki Kakehi of Keio University, has succeeded in fabricating a millimeter-scale light emitter with a built-in LED that flies through three-dimensional space, intended for touchable mid-air displays. Because it glows like a firefly, they named it "Luciola" (Note 5) after the scientific name of the Genji firefly.  
Previous small levitated objects using focused ultrasonic beams could levitate and move precisely and silently, but they carried no electronic circuits and were limited to extremely light objects such as polystyrene foam balls a few millimeters or less in diameter.  
By eliminating the battery through wireless power transfer and integrating the wireless-power receiver circuit needed to light the LED into a dedicated IC, the group achieved the necessary reduction in size and weight and succeeded in making a hemispherical light emitter 4 millimeters in diameter and 16 milligrams in weight that moves through the air. As the world's first "small light emitter with built-in electronics that moves through the air", it is expected to serve as a light-emitting pixel for touchable mid-air displays.  
The details of this research were published online in the journal Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies (IMWUT) on Tuesday, January 9 (Japan time; Monday, January 8, US Eastern time).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**4. Details:**  
Mid-air displays, which project three-dimensional images into the air, are being researched in many forms as the ultimate display. Optical methods using mirrors have been the mainstream, and the fact that the 3D image cannot be touched by hand has limited how real the viewer's experience can be.  
The group therefore developed a millimeter-scale light-emitting element that satisfies three requirements: it can move freely in three-dimensional space, it emits light by itself, and it can be touched. Because it glows like a firefly, they named it "Luciola" after the scientific name of the Genji firefly.  
Realizing Luciola requires two technologies: levitating and moving an object in mid-air, and supplying energy to the levitated LED. Here, focused ultrasonic beams were used for levitation and movement, and wireless power transfer for energy supply.  
Two ultrasonic arrays, each 17 centimeters square and consisting of 17 × 17 ultrasonic speakers at 40 kilohertz, inaudible to the human ear, arranged in a two-dimensional grid, are placed facing each other 20 centimeters apart. By controlling the phase of the electrical signal driving each speaker, the ultrasonic beam is focused on a single point in the space between the two arrays (Figure 1(a)). When an object is placed at this focal point, it levitates. Moving the focal point by controlling the phases of the speakers moves the object through the air with millimeter precision.  
Because an object levitated by ultrasound must be small and light, previous work was limited to objects a few millimeters or less in diameter with no electronic circuits, as light as polystyrene foam balls. Adding a battery to make the levitated object emit light would make it large and heavy, so that it could no longer be levitated by ultrasound.  
By eliminating the battery through wireless power transfer and integrating the electronic circuit needed to light the LED into a dedicated IC, the group achieved the necessary reduction in size and weight and succeeded in making a hemispherical light emitter 4 millimeters in diameter and 16 milligrams in weight that moves through the air (Figure 1(b)). This "small light emitter with built-in electronics that moves through the air" is a world first.  
Energy is supplied by magnetic resonance coupling wireless power transfer at 12.3 megahertz, from a power-transmitting coil 31 millimeters in diameter placed near the emitter to a receiving coil formed on a flexible printed circuit board (Note 6) built into the emitter (Figure 1(b)(c)). In wireless power transfer, the alternating voltage, which varies greatly with the relative position of the transmitting and receiving coils, must be rectified and regulated to the roughly 3-volt direct voltage needed to light the LED. Doing this with a combination of commercial general-purpose ICs made the circuit too large for the object to be levitated by ultrasound. By integrating the entire power supply circuit into a newly developed IC chip 1 millimeter square, the group succeeded in making it small and light (Figure 1(c)).  
As demonstrations of a light-emitting pixel for touchable mid-air displays, by synchronizing the timing of mid-air movement with switching the wireless power on and off, the LED is lit or extinguished according to its position in the air, so that characters and figures can be drawn in three-dimensional space (Figure 2), and a micro reading light can move through the air above a book following the reader's gaze (Figure 3).  
Next, to increase the expressive power of mid-air displays, the group plans to increase the number of light emitters and thus the number of light-emitting pixels. Furthermore, by adding sensors, actuators (Note 7) and wireless communication to small electronic objects that move through the air, they will pursue research extending them into the IoT field as small, mobile sensor nodes (Note 8).

**6. Glossary:**  
(Note 1) Focused ultrasonic beam: an ultrasonic beam narrowed to a fine focus.  
(Note 2) LED: light-emitting diode. A semiconductor element that emits light when a voltage is applied; used in lighting and elsewhere.  
(Note 3) Wireless power transfer: technology for transmitting and supplying electric power wirelessly, without a dedicated cable from the power source.  
(Note 4) IC: integrated circuit. An electronic component in which many elements such as transistors, resistors and capacitors are fabricated on a single silicon substrate to form an electronic circuit.  
(Note 5) Luciola: the genus name of the Genji firefly.  
(Note 6) Flexible printed circuit board: a thin, flexible substrate used to mount and wire electronic components.  
(Note 7) Actuator: a device that produces physical motion when energy is supplied, such as a motor.  
(Note 8) Sensor node: a small device that forms part of a sensor network, consisting of a battery, sensors, a wireless communication chip, an antenna, and IC boards for control, data logging and processing.
