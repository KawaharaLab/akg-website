## A 3D object-forming device that outputs in tens of seconds and can be reused

### Toward faster prototyping and design, and physical 3D displays

**1. Authors:**

- Yasuaki Kakehi (Associate Professor, Interfaculty Initiative in Information Studies, The University of Tokyo)
- Yoshihiro Kawahara (Associate Professor, Department of Information and Communication Engineering, Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, The University of Tokyo; Research Director, JST ERATO Kawahara Universal Information Network Project)
- Ryo Suzuki (Technical Assistant, Department of Information and Communication Engineering, Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, The University of Tokyo; second-year doctoral student, Department of Computer Science, University of Colorado Boulder)
- Junichi Yamaoka (Project Assistant Professor, Interfaculty Initiative in Information Studies, The University of Tokyo)

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**2. Key points:**

- We developed "Dynablock", a 3D object-forming device that can output an arbitrary three-dimensional shape in seconds to tens of seconds using about 3,000 blocks, 9 millimeters in size, with embedded magnets.
- Stacked blocks are pushed up from below by a linear actuator array (Note 1) and joined by magnetic force, forming three-dimensional shapes that are both fast to build and reusable. (Figure 1)
- It is expected to speed up prototyping in manufacturing and design, and in the longer term to be applied to physical 3D displays (Note 2).

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**3. Summary:**  
In recent years, digital fabrication technologies (Note 4), centered on 3D printers (Note 3), have attracted great attention as a new way of making things. However, outputting a solid object takes tens of minutes to hours, and once formed, an object cannot be reused as material the way clay can. These have been major limitations.  
To solve these problems, a research group led by Associate Professor Yasuaki Kakehi and Associate Professor Yoshihiro Kawahara of the University of Tokyo developed "Dynablock", a 3D object-forming device that quickly forms arbitrary three-dimensional shapes by assembling 9-millimeter blocks with embedded magnets (Figure 2). In this work, about 3,000 blocks stacked in eight layers are pushed up from below by a linear actuator array and joined, a mechanism for a fast and reusable 3D object-forming device that operates in seconds to tens of seconds.  
Dynablock's characteristics are expected to speed up prototyping and design in manufacturing. Further, by shortening the forming time even more, the group aims at applications to physical 3D displays.  
The details of this research will be presented at the "ACM Symposium on User Interface Software and Technology (UIST) 2018", held in Berlin, Germany, from Sunday, October 14 to Wednesday, October 17, 2018.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**4. Details:**  
In recent years, digital fabrication technologies such as 3D printers have moved beyond prototyping tools for manufacturing and are beginning to be applied in many fields, including design, education and welfare.  
When outputting three-dimensional objects in particular, however, forming takes time, and once an object is formed it cannot be remade on the spot or its material reused, as clay can. This work therefore proposes "dynamic 3D printing", which forms solids instantly and allows the material to be reused. In this method, small block-shaped material is assembled vertically and horizontally to form an arbitrary three-dimensional shape instantly (for example, a 3D object about 10 centimeters in length, width and height can be formed in seconds to tens of seconds).  
Previous methods for forming dynamic 3D shapes by combining small block-shaped parts include stacking blocks with a robot arm, like building with toy blocks, but with a single robot the assembly time grows in proportion to the number of blocks. Methods using self-assembling small robots (Note 5), in which the blocks themselves move to change the overall shape, have also been proposed, but each block is several centimeters or larger, assembly takes time, and the cost per block is high.  
In this dynamic 3D printing, blocks stacked inside the device are pushed upward by a two-dimensional array of linear actuators and joined to one another automatically, achieving fast assembly. Each cross-section of the output object is pushed up one layer at a time, and because a layer takes less than a second, a 3D object of several layers can be formed in seconds to tens of seconds. When an object is no longer needed, pushing it down separates the blocks, so the material can be reused (Figure 3).  
As a prototype of this method, the group developed Dynablock, which outputs arbitrary three-dimensional shapes in seconds to tens of seconds using about 3,000 blocks 9 millimeters in size. Each block has embedded magnets and can join to others horizontally and vertically. The blocks are stacked in eight layers and pushed up from below by 24 × 16 actuators (Figure 4, right). Partition plates around the linear actuators prevent horizontally adjacent blocks from joining inside the device, but once the actuators push the blocks up onto the stage, they leave the partitions and adjacent blocks are joined by magnetic force (Figure 5, bottom). The vertical magnets are weaker than the horizontal ones, so that when the blocks are pushed down, as in Figure 5, the joints separate. With this mechanism, 3D solids from furniture such as desks and chairs (Figure 6) to airplane-like shapes (Figure 4) can be output in 10 to 20 seconds. With smaller magnets, the proposed method allows individual blocks to be shrunk to 3 millimeters with the current design.  
Dynablock's characteristics are expected to speed up prototyping and design in manufacturing. Next, by building sensors, LEDs, IC chips and the like into individual blocks, the group aims to form functional objects whose color, texture and shape change dynamically with their surroundings, and, by shortening the forming time further, to apply the method to physical 3D displays.

**6. Glossary:**  
(Note 1) Linear actuator array: linear actuators, which convert the rotation of a motor into linear motion to move up and down, arranged in rows and columns.  
(Note 2) Physical 3D display: a device that displays information not as an image, like a computer screen or hologram, but as a physical solid that can actually be touched and changes dynamically.  
(Note 3) 3D printer: a device for making three-dimensional shapes. Methods include fused deposition modeling, which melts plastic with heat and solidifies it layer by layer, and stereolithography, which cures resin with light.  
(Note 4) Digital fabrication technology: a general term for computer-controlled machine tools and the technology that controls them. By electronically specifying and controlling the position and feed rate of machining, accurate physical shapes can be produced from digital data.  
(Note 5) Self-assembling small robots: a method, and the robots that implement it, in which block-shaped robots a few centimeters in size move by themselves and join with other robots to form an overall shape.
