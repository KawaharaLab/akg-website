### **A cuttable wireless charging sheet – a method that lets anyone make wirelessly chargeable products with ease**

**2. Key points:**

- We developed a wireless charging (Note 1) sheet that keeps working even when cut into any shape you like.
- The cuttable wireless charging sheet combines coil-array wiring that stays robust when the sheet is cut with control that avoids magnetic interference between neighboring coils.
- We expect that wirelessly chargeable products can be made easily just by cutting the sheet to fit everyday items such as furniture, bags and clothing and attaching it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**3. Summary:**

When wireless charging coils are built into products such as furniture and clothing, the usual approach has been to decide the product's shape first and then design and implement a coil array to match. With such coil arrays, however, the wiring must fit the shape and the coils must be placed and designed with magnetic interference in mind, which requires knowledge of high-frequency circuits and a great deal of time and effort.

A research group led by Associate Professor Yoshihiro Kawahara of the Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, The University of Tokyo, developed a sheet that provides wireless charging simply by cutting it into the shape the user wants and attaching it. The cuttable wireless charging sheet combines a wiring layout that loses as little of the coil array's function as possible when cut with control that avoids magnetic interference between neighboring coils.

As a demonstration of the proposed system, the group fabricated a wireless charging sheet weighing 82 g on a flexible printed circuit board (Note 2) measuring 400 mm × 400 mm. The sheet transfers power between coils using magnetic fields from several hundred kHz to several MHz, which are considered to have little effect on the human body, and can deliver up to about 5 W. Being easy to cut into various shapes and attach, it is expected to be used in desks and shelves that charge a smartphone simply placed on them, and in bags and clothing that charge electronic devices simply placed in a pocket.

The details of this research were published online in the journal Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies (IMWUT) on Friday, December 28, 2018 (Japan time; Thursday, December 27, US Eastern time).

**4. Details:**

In recent years, wireless charging systems using electromagnetic induction (Note 3) and magnetic resonance coupling (Note 4) have come into practical use one after another. However, the current wireless charging standards specified by the Wireless Power Consortium (WPC, Note 5) and the AirFuel Alliance (Note 6) are limited to transmission between a transmitter containing at most a few transmitting coils and a receiving coil placed directly above it, so charging works only in a small area. The ideal is an environment in which coils are built into the desks, shelves, walls and other furniture and interiors found everywhere, rather than into chargers on which only specific devices can be placed, so that every kind of electronic device, from mobile devices and sensors to appliances and lighting, is powered automatically. Various wireless charging sheets have been proposed, but all of them had to be designed and implemented for a particular shape, and cutting even part of the wiring disabled other parts of the transmitter or the whole of it. The wireless charging sheet therefore had to be remade for each shape of furniture or clothing, which took time and effort. Magnetic resonance coupling in particular suffers a large drop in efficiency from magnetic interference between neighboring coils, making fabrication even more costly.

The proposed wireless charging sheet, based on magnetic resonance coupling, gives existing furniture, clothing and the like wireless charging capability when cut to match their shape and attached (Figure 1). Unlike the approach of arranging only as many coils as the shape needs, its novelty lies in removing unneeded coils from a prefabricated, standard coil array.

When a sheet is cut and attached to existing furniture or clothing, it is cut from the outside. Most previous wireless charging sheets wired their coil arrays in a matrix, so cutting disabled some of the coils connected in rows and columns. The group therefore placed the power supply at the center of the sheet, where it will not be cut away, and wired from the center outward using an H-tree layout (Figure 2). With this wiring, current still reaches the coil array after cutting. Because an H-tree makes the wiring length from the supply to every coil equal, it also suppresses the variation in the impedance (Note 7) of each coil as seen from the supply, a characteristic concern of high-frequency circuits.

Placing coils as densely as possible allows a receiver placed anywhere on the sheet to be powered. On the other hand, the closer the coils are, the stronger the magnetic interference between them, which harms efficiency. The group therefore used time-division power transfer, in which coils switched on at the same time are kept at a distance from one another (Figure 3). The coil array is divided into groups of coils that are not adjacent to one another, and power is transferred group by group in turn, avoiding magnetic interference between neighboring coils. By combining H-tree wiring with time-division power transfer, the system makes the most of wireless charging on the sheet after it has been cut.

With this method, wireless charging can easily be added to everyday items such as furniture (Figure 4), bags and clothing (Figure 5) and storage boxes (Figure 6). The group will next use printed electronics to print the wiring and components of the sheet in a single step.

**6. Glossary:**

(Note 1) Wireless charging: technology for transmitting and supplying electric power wirelessly, without a dedicated cable from the power source.  
(Note 2) Flexible printed circuit board: a thin, flexible substrate used to mount and wire electronic components.  
(Note 3) Electromagnetic induction: one method of wireless charging. Power is sent wirelessly from a transmitting coil to a receiving coil using electromagnetic induction between coils.  
(Note 4) Magnetic resonance coupling: one method of wireless charging. Power is transferred using resonant coils. Compared with electromagnetic induction, it transfers power over longer distances and tolerates misalignment between the transmitting and receiving coils.  
(Note 5) Wireless Power Consortium (WPC): an organization that mainly specifies electromagnetic induction (the Qi standard).  
(Note 6) AirFuel Alliance: an organization that mainly specifies magnetic resonance coupling.  
(Note 7) Impedance: resistance in an alternating-current circuit.
