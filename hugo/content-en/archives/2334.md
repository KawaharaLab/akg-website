## Wireless charging anywhere in a room

### Toward IoT systems whose batteries never run out

**1. Authors:**

- Yoshihiro Kawahara (Professor, Department of Electrical Engineering and Information Systems, Graduate School of Engineering, The University of Tokyo / Research Director, JST ERATO Kawahara Universal Information Network Project)
- Takuya Sasatani (Second-year doctoral student, Department of Information and Communication Engineering, Graduate School of Information Science and Technology, The University of Tokyo)

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**2. Key points:**

- We devised and implemented a transmitter structure called the multimode quasistatic cavity resonator (multimode QSCR) and demonstrated wireless charging (Note 1) across an entire 3 m × 3 m room.
- Because the transmitting mechanism is embedded in the walls and floor and generates an alternating magnetic field (Note 2) distributed in three dimensions, there is no need to install structures such as conductor rods inside the room, as previous methods required.
- Because it can deliver several tens of watts over a wide area, it is expected to lead to IoT systems whose batteries never run out.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**3. Summary:**

With the rise of the Internet of Things (IoT), the number of electronic devices in our surroundings keeps growing. These devices need regular charging or battery replacement, and the cost of supplying power to each of them is becoming a real problem.

A research group led by Professor Yoshihiro Kawahara of the Graduate School of Engineering, The University of Tokyo, devised and implemented a transmitter structure called the multimode quasistatic cavity resonator (multimode QSCR) and demonstrated wireless charging of devices at any position in a room. The quasistatic cavity resonator (QSCR, Note 3), presented in 2017, was a new approach to wireless charging covering a large three-dimensional space, which had been considered difficult, but it required a large conductor rod at the center of the room and its magnetic field distribution was uneven. Noting that current on a metal plate can flow in several directions, the group devised the multimode QSCR, a new approach that can generate several magnetic field distributions, and showed by theoretical analysis that highly efficient wireless charging across an entire room and operation without a conductor rod are possible. In this work, the group further implemented a room-scale (3 m × 3 m) multimode QSCR and demonstrated wireless charging across the entire room. Because the method can deliver several tens of watts over a wide area, it is expected to lead to IoT systems whose batteries never run out.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

**4. Details:**

As the IoT develops, electronic devices with built-in functions such as interfaces, sensors and actuators are multiplying. Today most of these devices are powered by batteries or power cables, but the cost of replacing batteries and the tangle of wiring grow with the number of devices. Technology that supplies energy autonomously to many devices is therefore thought to be a key to realizing the ultimate IoT, in which everything around us is connected through networks and operates in concert. Wireless charging technologies, beginning with magnetic resonance coupling (Note 4) proposed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in 2007, have been expected to provide this autonomous energy supply, but supplying power safely, autonomously and efficiently to small devices at various positions in a large space has been difficult.

As a new approach to wireless charging over a large space, the quasistatic cavity resonator (QSCR) was proposed in 2017. It turns the room itself into a transmitter built from metal plates and conductor rods, generating an alternating magnetic field distributed in three dimensions inside the room and charging devices in it wirelessly. However, it required a large conductor rod at the center of the room, and charging efficiency dropped near the walls. To solve these problems, the group noted that current on a metal plate can flow in several directions and devised the multimode quasistatic cavity resonator (multimode QSCR). Theoretical analysis showed that highly efficient wireless charging is possible throughout the space and that operation without the central conductor rod is possible. In this work, the group implemented this multimode QSCR structure at a 3 m × 3 m scale and demonstrated it as a wireless charging system.

The room-wide wireless charging system consists of a room-sized multimode QSCR (transmitter), shown in Figure 1, and the receiver shown in Figure 2. The room-sized transmitter is built from metal plates and capacitors and operates as a low-loss transmitting resonator that generates an alternating magnetic field distributed within the structure. When an alternating magnetic field oscillating at the resonant frequency of this resonator is fed in through a drive coil, the oscillation spreads throughout the room. Energy is then exchanged between the electrical energy stored in the capacitors and the magnetic energy stored in the space, producing resonance. As a result, an alternating current flows across the walls and floor, generating a three-dimensional alternating magnetic field that fills the entire room (Figure 3). Finally, a coil resonator (Note 5) on the receiver, tuned to the same resonant frequency as the room, receives power through this magnetic field (Figure 4).

In experiments, the group confirmed that power could be delivered to receivers placed at various positions in the room. Wireless charging of devices far smaller than the charging area is known to be very difficult, but this work demonstrated power delivery at almost every position to a receiver 1/1000 the size of the charging area (by area).

The group will next work on extending the charging range and improving the system's efficiency. It will also explore easy ways of introducing the mechanism into real environments and pursue applications to actual IoT systems (Figure 5).

**6. Glossary:**

(Note 1) Wireless charging: technology for charging electronic devices without cables.

(Note 2) Alternating magnetic field: a magnetic field whose direction reverses periodically. In this method and in magnetic resonance coupling (Note 4), power is transferred through this alternating magnetic field.

(Note 3) Quasistatic cavity resonator (QSCR): a resonator structure presented in 2017, inspired by cavity resonators. It generates in a space an alternating magnetic field that interacts little with living bodies and the like.

(Note 4) Magnetic resonance coupling: a wireless charging technology presented by MIT in 2007 that uses coil resonators.

(Note 5) Coil resonator: a resonator made by connecting a coil and a capacitor to generate a magnetic field.
