Yukiya Mita (M2) has been selected for JST's ACT-X, a program that supports young researchers with original, ambitious ideas. We asked him what he wants to take on and pursue!

What is ACT-X?  
One of the Strategic Basic Research Programs run by JST (Japan Science and Technology Agency). It supports research aimed at creating new value, with the goal of discovering and nurturing outstanding young researchers. Its features are guidance from the research supervisor and area advisors and opportunities to build networks with researchers in other fields.

**– First, please tell us about your specialty and the research you are working on.**  
My main specialty is indoor acoustic positioning. It is something like an indoor version of GPS: a speaker emits a sound, a smartphone or similar device receives it, and by analyzing the features of that sound we compute where we are indoors.

**– What got you interested in this field?**  
When I heard about various research themes from my mentors, indoor acoustic positioning interested me the most. At first the focus was ranging, estimating the distance to a speaker, but as I thought about using the complex reflections indoors to identify two-dimensional coordinates even from a single speaker, my interest kept growing.

**– The theme selected for ACT-X this time, "An interactive AI system that realizes mutual understanding between the performer's expressive intent and the structure of the piece", is a new challenge one step beyond your specialty, we hear.**  
I have played the piano since I was small, and music is both a hobby and a lifelong pursuit for me. Wondering whether I could connect it with research is what led me to take on this project.

## **Taking on ACT-X: an AI accompaniment system with a sense of playing together**

**– Could you tell us concretely what the research is?**  
I want to build an accompaniment system that follows me when I play. It is also a system I personally wished I had.  
When practicing a piano concerto, for example, it is hard to arrange opportunities to fit your own part in while listening in real time to the other performers, the orchestra or the accompanist. Playing while listening to the others is a completely different experience from playing alone, so I found it difficult to practice in a way that matches the real performance. I wanted to practice with accompaniment that follows my playing and adjust to it as I listen. Existing systems exist, but my goal is to make something with more of a "sense of playing together".

**– What elements will you bring into the AI to heighten that sense of playing together?**  
The important thing is not simply to play as written, but to reflect the performer's nuances. I want to add, as the essence of the accompaniment, information about how the performer interprets the score, what sound they want to produce, and what sound is actually being produced.  
Markings in the score, such as "dolce" (sweetly) or "risoluto" (resolutely), are expressed differently by different people. I want the AI to extract those nuances well from information about what sound the performer produces in real time.

**– Why did you focus on nuance?**  
Fundamentally, I have a strong interest in tone. Going to concerts by many different performers, I always felt that even in the same hall, on the same piano, the sound differs completely from person to person. I had long wanted a mechanism to read those differences from the acoustic side, and that became the inspiration for this research.

**– What led you to apply to ACT-X with this new theme?**  
Because the approach differs from my usual specialty (indoor acoustic positioning), there was nowhere I could easily ask for advice if I tried to proceed alone. ACT-X has a community of people in the same area, and I heard that someone working on music had been selected last year, so I felt it would be a place where discussion would be easy.  
I feel that ACT-X is relatively open to research that is close to an application of what you are interested in, and to challenging themes.

**– Please tell us how you plan to proceed, and what you especially want to work on.**  
The theme only started in October, and honestly, I am at a stage where I am thinking hard about it.  
The first thing I want to tackle is the mapping between the performer's intent and the resulting sound. If I can collect data with the cooperation of many performers, this mapping may be achievable.  
Beyond that, I want feedback from the listener's side: what impression the performance gave. I would like to start by analyzing whether the tone the performer tried to produce matches the tone the listener receives, or whether it is received in a completely different way.

## **The driving force behind the research: a sensibility honed at the piano and the joy of trial and error**

**– You have been playing the piano for a long time.**  
Yes, about 18 years, since I was six. Since entering university I have kept it up modestly, but until this summer I practiced about three hours a day. I also play to relax. I get bored easily, so if I only play the piano I want to do something else, and as a result the balance with research may work out well.  
I listen to a lot of music too. While working it is often orchestral music. Lately I like "Zutto Mayonaka de Iinoni." It is J-pop, but it is popular with my music friends too, and I think it may have rhythms and nuances of sound that connect to classical music.

**– Was there a time when you were torn between music and research?**  
There was, in high school. In music, most people become professional performers or teachers, but I sometimes get nervous at recitals, and my style at the piano is intuitive: rather than theory, I play what I heard the way I thought sounded good. After much thought, I chose my current path: to devote myself to scholarship and to keep the piano as a lifelong hobby.

Indoor acoustic positioning uses reflections from walls and ceilings, so it is closely related to room acoustics. Now that I have also been able to start research in the area of music, I hope to bring the understanding of acoustics and the sensibility I developed at the piano into my research.

**– What do you enjoy about doing research?**  
I am the type who does not mind work, so I feel happy when I am quietly collecting data and using my hands. And when the results match my hypothesis, nothing is better. I assume that things basically do not go well by default. Extracting clues to a solution, "maybe if I do this here it will work", through dozens or hundreds of rounds of trial and error is hard, but it is also what makes it interesting.

## **Research that creates everyday "joy": social implementation and outlook**

**– How do you imagine this AI accompaniment research being implemented in society?**  
The biggest point is for it to become a finished system so that people can easily enjoy playing together at home. Music is often played alone and may be a somewhat solitary hobby, but I would be glad if this system made a future in which the joy of ensemble playing is easy to reach. Ultimately I want to make a product or app through which everyone can experience accompaniment personalized to them.

**– How would you like to put indoor acoustic positioning to use?**  
I expect indoor acoustic positioning to be a field in great demand. The applications are wide-ranging: knowing your position inside a shopping mall and being guided to the store you want, or identifying which seat you are sitting in at a restaurant to improve service. If positioning can be done using background music, I think the barrier to introducing such systems will fall.

**– Both the AI music research and the indoor acoustic positioning research have the potential to connect directly to people's daily lives and joys. What are your goals and outlook as a researcher?**  
I want to continue research after finishing my master's degree. First, I will devote myself fully to the research selected for ACT-X. I want to pursue this new challenge with the goal of turning the research into products and services and putting it into practical use.
